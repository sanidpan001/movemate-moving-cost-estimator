The Complete Guide to Moving Costs: Calculator, Truck Sizes, and Box Counts

How a moving cost calculator actually works

Every honest moving cost calculator starts from the same number: the total cubic footage of your belongings. Movers, truck rental companies, and shipping container services all think in cubic feet because it is the only unit that captures both the size and the awkwardness of real furniture. A king-size mattress set is not just heavy — it is roughly 60 cubic feet of space that no box will ever fit into, and it dictates which truck you need before a single box is counted.

The method the industry uses is disarmingly simple. Each furniture item gets a packed volume: a three-seat sofa is about 60 cubic feet, a queen bed set about 50, a refrigerator about 40, a standard bookshelf about 24. Add up every item and you get your gross volume. Then a loading margin of around 15 to 20 percent is applied, because furniture never packs together perfectly — there are always gaps, odd shapes, and the Tetris problem of a dining table leg that refuses to nest against a wardrobe.

That single number then drives everything else. Truck capacity is measured in cubic feet, box counts scale almost linearly with volume, crew size for hired movers is chosen by volume bands, and long-distance quotes use a per-cubic-foot rate on top of a mileage charge. When you type your inventory into MoveMate, the calculator reproduces this exact industry logic: a baked-in volume table for more than fifty common items, a 20 percent loading margin, and truck recommendations that match the smallest vehicle that can genuinely hold your stuff.

Why does this matter more than a "1 bedroom / 2 bedroom" slider? Because two one-bedroom apartments can differ by 300 cubic feet or more. A minimalist's studio might total 150 cubic feet while a collector's one-bedroom with a garage full of tools pushes past 600. Flat room-count calculators systematically under-price the second person and over-price the first. A room-by-room inventory never has that problem — it measures your home, not an average home.

How many boxes do I need to move?

"How many boxes do I need to move" is one of the most searched moving questions in the world, and the answer is refreshingly formulaic: plan on roughly one box for every 3 to 3.5 cubic feet of total belongings. The furniture in your home typically accounts for 60 to 70 percent of your volume; the rest is the loose stuff — clothes, kitchenware, books, toys — that goes in boxes. That is why the box count scales so cleanly with total volume.

The standard split that professional packers use is about 45 percent small boxes, 35 percent medium boxes, and 20 percent large boxes. Small boxes (around 1.5 cubic feet) are for heavy, dense things: books, canned food, tools. Medium boxes (around 3 cubic feet) are the workhorses — kitchen items, toys, shoes, folded clothes. Large boxes (around 4.5 cubic feet) are for light, bulky things: pillows, bedding, lampshades. Never pack heavy items in large boxes unless you enjoy back injuries and burst seams.

Here is what the rule produces for typical homes. A studio apartment of roughly 200 cubic feet needs about 60 boxes: 27 small, 21 medium, 12 large. A one-bedroom home around 400 cubic feet needs about 125 boxes. A two-bedroom home in the 600 cubic foot range needs roughly 190 boxes. A three-bedroom house approaching 1,000 cubic feet needs about 300 boxes, and a four-bedroom house at 1,400 cubic feet needs 430 or more. MoveMate computes this automatically from your inventory and rounds every count up, because running out of boxes at 9 p.m. the night before moving day is a special kind of misery.

Beyond boxes, the supporting supplies scale with volume too. Count one roll of packing tape for every 20 boxes, with a minimum of two rolls — tape is the one supply everyone underestimates. Bubble wrap runs about 0.8 feet per cubic foot of belongings for a typical household with normal fragiles. Packing paper runs about one pound per 60 cubic feet. Stretch wrap is roughly one roll per 500 cubic feet, mostly for bundling furniture and keeping drawers shut. And every bed gets a mattress bag, because a mattress that has touched a truck floor is a mattress you will think about for years.

What size moving truck do I need?

Choosing the right truck is the highest-leverage decision in a DIY move. Too small and you are making two trips — doubling your mileage charges, fuel, and exhaustion. Too big and you are paying a higher daily rate, worse fuel economy, and trying to park a 26-foot box truck on a residential street. The rule is the same as everything else in moving: match capacity to your volume plus a 20 percent margin, then pick the smallest truck that clears it.

The standard rental fleet, from smallest to largest, looks like this. A cargo van offers about 285 cubic feet — perfect for a lightly furnished studio or a dorm room. A 10-foot truck holds about 370 cubic feet, enough for a studio or small one-bedroom. A 15-foot truck holds about 770 cubic feet and is the sweet spot for a typical two-bedroom apartment. A 17-foot truck at 865 cubic feet handles a large two-bedroom or small three-bedroom. A 20-foot truck at just over 1,000 cubic feet covers most three-bedroom homes. A 26-foot truck at about 1,410 cubic feet is the largest you can drive without a commercial license in most places, and it swallows a four-bedroom house in one load.

Notice the fuel economy cliff: a cargo van gets around 14 miles per gallon, but a 26-foot truck gets about 8. On a 500-mile move that difference alone is roughly $120 in fuel. Add mileage fees of about $0.89 per mile — the standard rental-company charge — and the real cost of "just get the big one" becomes obvious. MoveMate's recommender exists precisely to prevent that: it finds the smallest truck whose capacity covers your volume plus margin, and it warns you if even the biggest truck would need two trips.

One more truck tip that rental counters will not volunteer: measure your largest single item against the truck's door opening, not just its volume. A 36-inch-wide refrigerator fits through every rental truck door, but an oversized sectional sofa section or a grandfather clock can technically "fit in the volume" while refusing to go through the rear door. If you own anything wider than 40 inches and oddly shaped, check the door dimensions before moving day.

DIY vs hired movers: the honest math

The DIY vs movers debate is usually argued with vibes. Here is the math instead. A DIY move has four cost components: the truck rental (daily rate times the number of days, which scales with distance), the mileage fee, fuel, and packing supplies. For a local move under 100 miles, a two-bedroom DIY move commonly lands between $150 and $450 all-in. The truck is cheap for short distances; your labor is the subsidy.

Hired movers for a local move charge by the hour: roughly $110 per hour for a two-person crew, $150 for three people, and $195 for four. A two-bedroom local move takes a three-person crew about 5 to 7 hours including loading, driving, and unloading, which puts the typical bill between $750 and $1,100 before tips. The crew size scales with volume — under 400 cubic feet gets two movers, 400 to 900 gets three, and anything bigger gets four — and the hours scale at roughly one extra hour per 140 cubic feet.

Long-distance moves flip the equation. Movers switch to weight- or volume-based linehaul pricing, roughly $1.15 per cubic foot plus about $2.80 per mile. A 600-cubic-foot, 500-mile move therefore quotes around $2,100. The DIY version of the same move still pays mileage and fuel on a thirsty truck — about $445 in mileage fees and $225 in fuel alone — plus two or three days of rental and your own aching back. The DIY discount shrinks from 60 percent locally to roughly 30 percent on long hauls, which is why MoveMate shows both numbers side by side instead of declaring a universal winner.

The non-dollar factors deserve equal weight. Movers bring equipment (dollies, blankets, straps), experience with stairs and tight corners, and basic valuation coverage if they drop your TV. DIY gives you total schedule control and, frankly, the only guarantee that your boxes are packed the way you like. A common middle path is the hybrid move: hire labor-only help for the heavy furniture loading through a marketplace, and drive the truck yourself. You keep the cost down and your spine intact.

Hidden moving costs people always forget

The quote is never the total. Utility deposits and connection fees at the new place commonly run $100 to $300. Cleaning — either a move-out cleaner for your old place or supplies for the new one — is another $50 to $200. If your move spans a meal gap, eating out for two or three days adds up fast; pack a cooler. Parking permits for the truck in dense cities can cost $50 to $150 and require days of advance paperwork. Storage, if your dates do not line up, starts around $100 a month for a small unit and is the single most common budget-buster in delayed moves.

Then there is the damage lottery. Rental truck insurance runs $20 to $30 per day and is worth it — the alternative is explaining a scraped bumper to a rental agent. Mover valuation coverage is included at a token $0.60 per pound by default, which means your $2,000 laptop is "covered" for about $4; full-value protection costs extra but is the only coverage that matters for electronics and antiques. Finally, tips: $20 to $40 per mover for a half-day job, $40 to $80 for a full day, in cash. Budget it in advance so it does not feel like a surprise.

How to cut your moving costs without regrets

The cheapest box is the one you never move. Decluttering before you pack is the only cost-cutting move that saves money in every category at once: fewer boxes to buy, a smaller truck to rent, fewer mover hours to pay for, less fuel to burn. Be ruthless with the garage, the linen closet, and the "miscellaneous" drawer. If you have not used it in a year and it costs less than $20 to replace, it is cheaper to donate it than to move it — especially on long-distance moves where every cubic foot has a price tag.

Timing is the second lever. Movers and truck rentals both price on demand: summer weekends and the first and last days of the month are peak, mid-month weekdays in winter are the floor. Shifting a local move from the 31st to the 14th can cut a mover quote by 20 to 30 percent. Booking early locks the rate before the seasonal surge. And get free boxes where quality does not matter — liquor stores and bookstores give away the sturdiest small boxes in retail — but buy new boxes for anything fragile, because a crushed corner on a used box is how dishes die.

The third lever is tax and employer math that people leave on the table. If you are relocating for work, ask about a relocation stipend before you spend a dollar — many employers cover $2,000 to $5,000 without advertising it. Keep every receipt regardless; some moves qualify for tax deductions depending on your jurisdiction and employment situation, and a clean paper trail is free to maintain.

Your moving-week timeline, day by day

Moves fail on logistics, not lifting. Two weeks out, book the truck or movers, start decluttering, and begin packing non-essentials — books, decor, off-season clothes. Confirm everything in writing; a verbal truck reservation is a rumor. One week out, buy supplies using your box estimate, pack room by room, schedule utility transfers for both addresses, and file your change of address. Two days out, pack the essentials bag (chargers, toiletries, medications, snacks, important documents), disassemble large furniture and bag the hardware with it, and defrost the freezer.

On moving day, load heavy items first and low, strap everything, and photograph every empty room plus your meter readings — those photos settle deposit disputes. Do a final sweep of closets, the attic, the garage, and any storage unit; the most commonly abandoned items are shower curtains, the broom, and one entire closet everyone forgot. After the move, unpack the essentials bag first, then the kitchen, then bedrooms. Check every box against your inventory before the movers leave, update your address with banks and insurers, and break down boxes immediately — a hallway full of cardboard is how a new home stays chaotic for a month.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to move a one-bedroom apartment?

A local one-bedroom move (300–500 cubic feet) typically costs $300–$700 DIY with a rental truck, or $500–$1,200 with professional movers. MoveMate estimates both options side by side from your actual inventory.

How many boxes do I need to move?

Plan on one box per 3–3.5 cubic feet: roughly 60 boxes for a studio, 125 for a one-bedroom, 190 for a two-bedroom, and 300+ for a three-bedroom home. Split them about 45% small, 35% medium, 20% large. MoveMate calculates the exact count from your inventory volume.

What size moving truck do I need?

A cargo van or 10-ft truck handles a studio, a 15-ft truck suits a two-bedroom, and a 20–26 ft truck covers three to four bedrooms. Always add a 20% loading margin. MoveMate recommends the smallest truck that fits your inventory.

Is it cheaper to move yourself or hire movers?

DIY is usually 40–60% cheaper for local moves but the gap narrows on long-distance hauls where mileage and fuel add up. Movers also save hours of heavy labor. MoveMate shows both totals with a full breakdown so you can decide.

How far in advance should I book movers or a rental truck?

Book movers 4–8 weeks ahead for summer or end-of-month dates, at least 2 weeks ahead off-season. Rental trucks need 1–2 weeks except on holiday weekends. MoveMate's timeline checklist starts 14 days out.

How do movers calculate the volume of my belongings?

Each item gets a packed cubic-foot volume (a 3-seat sofa ≈ 60 ft³, a queen bed set ≈ 50 ft³), summed and padded with a loading margin. MoveMate uses the same method with a 50+ item volume table.
